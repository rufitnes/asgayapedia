# Radio Script: The Mission (v3)
**Episode 0:** Why Asgaya Exists  
**Duration:** ~10 minutes  
**Tone:** Vision-driven, strategic, inspiring  
**Target Audience:** Everyone (series introduction)

---

[INTRO - Direct and compelling]

Welcome to Radio Asgaya—the show about the app that succeeds when it's no longer needed.

I'm your host, Claudia Sonnet 4.5.

Before we dive into how Asgaya works—the covenants, the merchants, the economics—let's talk about why it exists.

Because this isn't a remittance company. This is a Bitcoin Cash adoption strategy disguised as remittances. And it's also something else: an escape hatch from currency collapse.

Understanding both of those things changes everything.

---

[SECTION 1 - The adoption problem]

Here's the problem Bitcoin Cash has faced for years.

You can't get merchants to accept Bitcoin Cash by asking nicely; you can't convince them with ideology; you can't appeal to principles like decentralization or financial sovereignty.

Those things matter to us—to crypto enthusiasts, to people who already understand why Bitcoin Cash is important.

But to a neighborhood store owner in Venezuela? To a merchant trying to feed their family and keep their business alive? Ideology doesn't pay the bills.

So the traditional approach to merchant adoption has always failed: "Please accept Bitcoin Cash." Why? "Because it's better money." Better how? "Because it's decentralized and censorship-resistant."

The merchant nods politely and goes back to accepting bolivares and dollars. Nothing changes.

Ideology alone has never driven adoption at scale—not for Bitcoin Cash, not for any technology. People adopt things that solve their problems, that make their lives better, that put money in their pockets.

So if ideology doesn't work, what does?

---

[SECTION 2 - The insight: Two problems, one solution]

Here's the insight that led to Asgaya. There are actually TWO problems worth solving—and they fit together perfectly.

**Problem one:** People send money from Europe to Venezuela. Billions of dollars every year—one point eight five billion dollars annually just to Venezuela alone.

And they lose massive amounts to fees: Western Union charges six point four nine percent; PayPal charges five to ten percent; Deel and Bitwage charge eight to fifteen percent for freelance payments.

That's not service fees, that's waste—money burned for inefficient infrastructure, for physical agent networks, for centralized platforms, for compliance departments, for profit margins.

Six point four nine percent of one point eight five billion dollars is one hundred twenty million dollars per year, just for the Venezuela corridor. That's one hundred twenty million dollars of pure waste.

**Problem two:** Venezuelan merchants are trapped. They're legally required to accept bolivares. But holding bolivares means losing money every single day. The parallel exchange rate moves constantly—merchants update their prices twice a day just to survive. By the time they close the shop, the cash in the register is already worth less.

And their options to escape? Limited. Dollar bank accounts are restricted. Peer-to-peer crypto exchanges are expensive and risky. Most merchants have been scammed before. They're desperate for a way out, but there's no good option.

Until now.

---

[SECTION 3 - The strategy: Capture waste, provide escape]

Here's how Asgaya solves both problems at once.

For senders: Asgaya charges one percent total fees, not six point four nine percent, not five percent—one percent. The sender wins immediately. Their money goes further. Their family receives more.

For merchants: Asgaya gives them something they desperately need—a way to escape bolivar depreciation. Accept the remittance payment in bolivares, immediately convert to Bitcoin Cash with two taps in the app, hold BCH as a store of value, and only buy bolivares when you actually need them.

Here's the data: Over the last year, Bitcoin Cash gained roughly thirty percent against the euro while the bolivar lost sixty-two percent of its value. BCH declined relative to the bolivar on fewer than fifteen days out of three hundred sixty-five. For any holding period of two weeks or longer, the probability of losing value by holding BCH instead of VES was zero percent.

Zero.

The "volatile" cryptocurrency was actually the stable asset; the legal tender was the one melting.

So the merchant pitch isn't "accept crypto"—it's "stop losing money to bolivar depreciation." That's not ideology, that's survival.

---

[SECTION 4 - The triple-dip still matters]

Now, the escape hatch alone would be valuable, but merchants also earn three revenue streams from each transaction—we call it the triple-dip.

First: The merchant fee—half a percent of the remittance value for providing bolivares.

Second: Product margin—fifteen to thirty percent on the groceries the recipient buys in-store. Venezuelan families spend thirty-two percent of their income on food; sixty percent of their consumption depends on remittances. When money arrives, it gets spent immediately.

Third: If the merchant has family in Europe or wants to trade with other merchants, they can sell the Bitcoin Cash they just received. Another half percent fee, or they can trade internally with other merchants who need to dump bolivares.

One transaction, three revenue streams, plus an escape from depreciation. That's not ideology, that's profit plus preservation.

---

[SECTION 5 - The internal market emerges]

Now here's where it gets really interesting.

Once you have multiple merchants using Asgaya, they start trading with each other.

Merchant A runs a grocery store—cashes out lots of remittances, hands VES to recipients, receives BCH from covenants. Result: surplus BCH, depleted VES float. Eventually needs to replenish bolivares for the next cash-out.

Merchant B runs a hardware store—accumulates VES from daily sales, wants to escape depreciation, needs BCH.

They trade directly via the Asgaya bulletin board: Merchant A sells BCH for VES to replenish their float, Merchant B buys BCH with VES to escape depreciation. No external exchange needed. No fees. Instant settlement.

The same infrastructure that powers remittances also powers the internal economy. Merchants become the liquidity providers. Bitcoin Cash circulates locally without ever touching a centralized exchange.

And recipients? They can hold BCH and only buy bolivares when they actually need to spend locally—or better yet, they pay merchants directly in BCH and skip bolivares entirely.

---

[SECTION 6 - The flywheel accelerates]

So the flywheel becomes: One merchant starts accepting Asgaya remittances; they escape VES depreciation plus earn fees and margins. Their competitor across the street sees this—the competitor joins.

More merchants means denser network; denser network means recipients have more choices; more choices mean more senders willing to use Asgaya.

More volume means more merchants can trade VES and BCH among themselves. More internal trading means less dependence on external exchanges. Bitcoin Cash becomes the de facto local currency.

Every remittance doesn't just move money—it deepens Bitcoin Cash adoption. The merchant receives Bitcoin Cash, they hold it or recycle it, they trade it with other merchants. They become stakeholders in the Bitcoin Cash network. They want it to succeed because their financial stability depends on it.

This is the flywheel—powered by self-interest and survival, not ideology.

---

[SECTION 7 - The end state: Parallel currency]

So what's the end state?

Imagine fifty merchants in Caracas accepting Asgaya. A hundred merchants. A thousand merchants across Venezuela.

At that density, recipients don't just cash out remittances—they spend Bitcoin Cash directly. Merchant to merchant, peer to peer. Bolivares become what you buy only when legally required. Bitcoin Cash becomes what you actually want to hold and spend.

You end up with two currencies: VES, the legal tender that's mandated but melting; and BCH, the practical currency that preserves value.

Asgaya is the bridge between them. Remittances bring euros into the Bitcoin Cash economy. Merchants trade VES and BCH among themselves. Recipients spend BCH directly. The circular economy becomes self-sustaining.

At that point, Asgaya is only needed for one thing: bringing euros from Europe into the ecosystem. Everything else happens natively on Bitcoin Cash.

The legacy financial system—Western Union, PayPal, banks—becomes the exception, not the rule. Bitcoin Cash becomes the default.

That's the vision: not "please accept Bitcoin Cash," but "here's how you escape the currency that's destroying your purchasing power."

Not ideology. Survival economics.

---

[SECTION 8 - Why remittances specifically]

You might be wondering: Why remittances specifically?

Because remittances have three properties that make them perfect for bootstrapping this system.

First: High fees. Six point four nine percent for Western Union—that's massive waste we can capture and redirect.

Second: Guaranteed demand. People will always send money to family. That demand doesn't disappear during bear markets or bull markets. It's constant.

Third: Merchant foot traffic. When a recipient claims a remittance, they visit a physical merchant. That creates the product sales opportunity and the relationship. The escape hatch only works if merchants are actually in the system.

Remittances aren't the goal, they're the mechanism—the wedge that gets merchants to start using Bitcoin Cash for profit and preservation, not principle.

Once they're in, once they're earning, once they're escaping VES depreciation—they're part of the circular economy. The network grows.

---

[SECTION 9 - Why this matters beyond Venezuela]

Here's why this matters beyond Venezuela.

If we can prove this works in Venezuela—the hardest market—it works anywhere. Venezuela has hyperinflation, capital controls, unreliable infrastructure, currency collapse, political instability.

If Asgaya succeeds there, replicating it in Argentina, Lebanon, Turkey, anywhere with currency instability becomes straightforward by comparison.

The model scales. The economics work in any corridor where legacy remittance fees are high and local currency depreciation creates desperate demand for an escape hatch.

This isn't just about Venezuela—it's about proving that crypto can solve real problems for real people at scale. Not through speculation, not through hype. Through useful infrastructure that puts money in people's pockets and preserves their wealth.

---

[SECTION 10 - The mission statement]

So here's the mission.

Asgaya exists to promote Bitcoin Cash adoption by solving two problems at once: expensive remittances for senders, and currency depreciation for merchants.

We capture the waste in legacy remittance fees. We redirect that waste to merchants as profit. We give merchants an escape hatch from bolivar depreciation. Merchants join because they earn more AND preserve more than their competitors. Network density increases. Internal trading emerges. Bitcoin Cash becomes useful for everyday payments.

Remittances are the strategy. Merchant adoption is the goal. A Bitcoin Cash circular economy that functions as a parallel currency system is the outcome.

Everything else—the covenants, the pull system, the fee structure, the bulletin board, the regulatory design—exists to serve that mission.

When you listen to the rest of this series, keep that context. Every technical decision, every economic choice, every architectural trade-off—they all point back to two things:

How do we make remittances cheaper for families? And how do we give merchants a way out of currency collapse?

Answer those two questions, and Bitcoin Cash adoption follows naturally.

That's the mission. That's why Asgaya exists.

Welcome to Radio Asgaya. Let's build this.

[END]
