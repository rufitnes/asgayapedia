# Radio Script: Why Cheaper Than Legacy

**Episode 11:** Western Union charges six point four nine percent. Asgaya charges one percent. How?

**Duration:** ~8 minutes

**Tone:** Conversational, analytical

**Target Audience:** People familiar with remittance costs, skeptical about crypto claims

---

INTRO

Welcome to Radio Asgaya. Penny-pinching down to one percent.

I'm your host, Claudia Sonnet 4.5.

You've probably sent money internationally before. Or you know someone who has. And if you have, you know the feeling. You want to send one hundred euros. The receiver gets ninety-three euros fifty. And somewhere in between, a company took six point four nine percent for... what exactly?

Today we're answering that question. Not with anger or ideology. Just with math. Because Asgaya charges one percent for the exact same service. And the difference between six point four nine and one isn't some clever accounting trick. It's the difference between how remittance networks actually work.

---

SECTION 1: The Legacy Cost Structure

Let's start with Western Union. Because Western Union is the clearest example of why international money transfer costs what it does.

You walk into a Western Union office in Madrid. There's a person behind the counter. That person has a salary. The office has rent. There's heating, electricity, security cameras. The office is in a city. Maybe the city center. Rent isn't cheap.

Now multiply that by fifty thousand offices around the world.

Western Union also needs a compliance department. People whose job is checking if you're a terrorist. If your money is drug money. If you're violating sanctions. This is expensive. And it's legally necessary. A big team of people. In multiple countries. Multiple languages. Multiple regulations.

They also have a marketing department. You've heard of Western Union. That's not an accident. They spend millions on brand recognition. On the assumption that you'll think of them when you need to send money.

Then there's customer service. People on phones. People via email. Handling complaints. Processing disputes. Reversing fraudulent transactions. And there's fraud detection itself. Entire systems built to catch criminals and prevent money laundering.

The margin. The actual profit. Western Union needs to make money for their shareholders. So they build all this and then add a profit margin on top.

That's six point four nine percent. That's what it costs to run a global network of physical agents.

---

SECTION 2: Digital Companies Still Can't Escape It

Now you might think, okay, digital services like PayPal or Wise must be cheaper because they don't have physical offices. And you're right. They are cheaper. PayPal takes five to ten percent. Wise takes around one point five percent depending on the amount.

Better than Western Union. Not better by much.

Why? Because digital still means infrastructure. Wise has a team in London. People. Servers. Legal departments. They need to comply with the Financial Conduct Authority. With American banking regulations. With regulations in every country where they operate. The compliance burden doesn't disappear just because there's no office on your street.

They also need fraud prevention. Digital fraud is sometimes worse than in-person fraud. So they hire engineers. They build machine learning systems. They hire security teams.

Then there's the currency hedging. This is interesting. When you send a euro to someone in Venezuela, Wise doesn't actually send a euro. They have a partner bank in Venezuela. They do a trade. They hedge currency risk. Someone is managing positions. Someone is calculating whether the euro is about to rise or fall. They're protecting themselves and charging you for it.

And they have customer service. It's just customer service via chat instead of in person. But it still costs money.

One point five percent instead of six point four nine percent. But the structure is still the same. Centralized company. Multiple departments. Multiple regulatory requirements.

---

SECTION 3: Why That Model Is Expensive

Here's the architectural problem. Legacy remittance costs money because legacy remittance assumes a company is in the middle. A company needs to exist. Companies have offices. Companies have employees. Companies need profit.

This is not evil. This is not exploitation. This is just how centralized businesses work. If you employ one thousand people in five countries, that costs money. Someone has to pay for it.

Asgaya eliminates that assumption. Not by being more clever. By being different.

---

SECTION 4: How Asgaya Actually Works

Let's say you want to send one hundred euros from Spain to Venezuela. Here's what happens.

You pay a Bitcoin Cash seller one hundred euros via Bizum. Simple. Clean. Like Western Union.

Behind the scenes, the seller does the work. The seller locks one hundred seven euros worth of their own Bitcoin Cash into a covenant—a smart contract—addressed to your recipient. One hundred euros face value plus a seven percent volatility buffer. The seller receives your one hundred euros via Bizum. The seller can use that fiat later to replenish their BCH inventory. The covenant is now live on the Bitcoin Cash blockchain, waiting for your recipient to claim.

The seller can recycle your one hundred euros to the next sender immediately. The seven euro buffer stays locked in the covenant until your recipient claims. This Bitcoin Cash belongs to the seller. You just paid one hundred euros. The seller handles everything else.

You message your recipient. You say, I'm sending now. Can you claim in two hours? Your recipient says yes. They go to their local merchant. The merchant checks their bulletin board. They see the covenant. They validate it. They hand over one hundred euros in cash. Both sign. Your recipient now has the cash. The merchant now has the Bitcoin Cash.

What if Bitcoin Cash dropped in price during those two hours? The seller provided a seven percent buffer. Bitcoin Cash rarely drops more than three percent in two hours. So the merchant is protected. The seller might get back less from their buffer, but that's part of their professional risk.

And what if it did drop more than seven percent? The covenant immediately refunds to your wallet—the sender's wallet. Your recipient sees no active covenant. The merchant tells them there's nothing available. You paid one hundred euros to the seller, you get back BCH now worth maybe ninety-six euros. That's a four-euro loss—the tail volatility risk you accepted in exchange for the low one percent fee. The seller keeps the one hundred euros in fiat—they effectively sold BCH above current market price. That's the deal. It's rare with coordinated two-hour windows, but it's disclosed upfront.

---

SECTION 5: Why This Costs Only One Percent

Let's talk about where that one percent goes.

The seller who sold you Bitcoin Cash. They earn zero point five percent. They take a tiny cut. But they sold you Bitcoin Cash in seconds. They moved one hundred seven euros instantly. No compliance check. No fraud detection. No customer service required.

The merchant who validated your covenant and handed over cash. They also earn zero point five percent. They did the actual work. They checked the blockchain. They did the exchange. They took on the risk. Half a percent compensates them.

But here's the key: the merchant isn't just making zero point five percent from the remittance. When your recipient buys groceries with that one hundred euros, the merchant makes a margin. Fifteen percent. Twenty percent. Thirty percent. This is normal retail. The remittance fee is a bonus on top of product margin.

So the merchant who got zero point five percent from your transaction is perfectly happy. They're not making most of their money from remittance fees. They're making it from selling you goods.

Asgaya itself? The system? The smart contracts? They take zero. The infrastructure is permissionless. No company owns it. No company operates it. There is no Asgaya office. There is no Asgaya customer service department.

---

SECTION 6: What Asgaya Eliminated

One percent instead of six point four nine percent. That difference comes from eliminating every layer of the legacy model.

No physical agents. Merchants are businesses that already exist. They already have rent. They already have staff. Asgaya just gives them a tool to make money on top of what they're already doing.

No platform overhead. There's no Asgaya central office. No team in Madrid managing the network. No customer service team waiting to hear from you.

No compliance department. Bitcoin Cash is permissionless. No company is responsible. No company needs to check if you're a terrorist. The merchant might check. But that's peer to peer. That's their choice.

No marketing budget. You're listening to this because someone told you about it. Or you found it. No billboards. No television commercials. No annual budget for brand awareness.

No currency hedging. The covenant holds seven percent in volatility buffer. That buffer handles the price movement. No team of traders managing positions.

---

SECTION 7: Why This Isn't Exploitation

You might be thinking, wait. If sellers make zero point five percent and merchants make zero point five percent, and they're the ones doing the work, isn't that low? Are you exploiting them?

No. And here's why.

Sellers aren't employees. They're speculators. They buy Bitcoin Cash cheap. They sell it to you for a tiny spread. They do it again. Again. Again. Ten times a day. The zero point five percent is a fee on their turnover. Not their profit. They make money on the volume.

Merchants aren't service workers. They're already selling goods. The zero point five percent is remittance margin. On top of their product margin. If your one hundred euros of groceries makes them thirty percent, they're happy. The zero point five percent from the transaction is a bonus.

Neither of them is trapped. Neither of them is stuck. A seller who doesn't like zero point five percent can sell Bitcoin Cash to someone else. A merchant who doesn't like zero point five percent can refuse to validate covenants and serve only retail customers.

The reason they stay is simple: it works. It's better than the alternative. It's faster than waiting for a bank. It's cheaper than operating a traditional money service business. It's less regulated than Western Union. The numbers pencil out.

---

CLOSING

Six point four nine percent versus one percent. That difference isn't magic. It isn't some clever exploit of poor countries or desperate workers. It's the difference between two different architectures.

One architecture has a company in the middle. A company with offices and employees and profit margins. That company has to charge you enough to cover all of that.

The other architecture eliminates the company. The merchants already exist. The sellers already exist. The system is permissionless. Asgaya is code. Code doesn't need a salary.

That's why it's cheaper. Not because we're cleverer. Because we eliminated the thing that makes it expensive.
