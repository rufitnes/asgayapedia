# Radio Script: The Melting Currency Problem
**Episode 34:** When Everyone Wants to Escape  
**Duration:** ~12 minutes  
**Tone:** Empathetic, urgent, solution-focused

Today we're talking about a problem that's hard to understand if you've never lived through it—and impossible to ignore if you have: what it's like when the currency in your wallet is worth less every single hour.

This isn't theoretical. This isn't about distant economic policy. This is about a merchant who opens their shop at eight AM, checks the parallel dollar rate on their phone, updates the prices on their chalkboard, sells groceries all day, and closes at six PM with a cash register full of money that's already worth less than it was at noon.

This is the melting currency problem. And it changes everything about how people think about money, risk, and escape.

---

[SECTION 1 - A day in the life]

Let me walk you through a day.

You're a merchant. You own a small neighborhood store in Caracas. You wake up at six thirty AM, check your phone. The parallel dollar rate moved overnight—up two point five percent. That means the bolivar lost two point five percent of its value while you slept.

You open the shop at eight. You update your chalkboard. Rice: twenty-eight thousand bolivares per kilo. Cooking oil: forty-two thousand per liter. Beans: thirty-one thousand. You've done this calculation so many times you don't even need a calculator anymore.

A regular customer comes in at nine AM. "Is this today's price?" she asks. You nod. She knows the game. Everyone knows the game. She buys what she needs now, because tomorrow it'll be more expensive.

At noon, you check the rate again. Up another one percent. You don't update the prices yet—it's too soon. You'll wait until tomorrow morning.

By six PM when you close, you count the cash. Three million bolivares. Yesterday, that bought thirty-five kilograms of rice wholesale. Today? Thirty-three kilograms. Tomorrow? Probably thirty-one.

Your revenue didn't drop. Your purchasing power did.

This is the melting currency problem. The harder you work, the faster you run—and the money in your hand melts anyway.

---

[SECTION 2 - The numbers: What a year looks like]

Let's zoom out. What does a full year look like?

From May twenty twenty-five to May twenty twenty-six, the Venezuelan bolivar lost sixty-two percent of its value against the US dollar on the parallel market.

Here's what that means in practice: If you started the year with one million bolivares under your mattress, by the end of the year it had the purchasing power of three hundred eighty thousand bolivares.

You didn't spend it. You didn't lose it. You just... held it. And it melted.

And here's the truly brutal part: There were zero days—zero—where the bolivar strengthened. The parallel rate only moved in one direction: up. Which means the bolivar only moved in one direction: down.

Imagine running a business in that environment. You make a sale. You hold the cash. By evening, it's worth less. By tomorrow, less still. By next week, noticeably less.

You're not running a business—you're trying to outrun depreciation. And you can't. No one can. The depreciation is structural, continuous, guaranteed.

---

[SECTION 3 - The psychological trap]

So what do people do?

They spend fast. As soon as money arrives—whether it's from sales, from remittances, from a paycheck—people spend it. Immediately. Because holding it is losing it.

This creates a strange economy. Money circulates fast. Really fast. Not because people want to spend—but because they want to avoid holding.

You're a recipient. Your cousin in Spain sends you a remittance. One hundred eighty euros. You convert it to bolivares. You have three million bolivares in your hand.

What do you do? You don't save it. Saving bolivares is financial suicide. You buy groceries. You buy medicine. You buy phone credit. You buy anything that holds value better than cash.

You're a merchant. A customer just handed you three hundred thousand bolivares for groceries. What do you do? You don't hold it. You restock inventory as soon as possible. You pay your supplier in cash. You convert it to dollars if you can—but most merchants can't, not easily, not without getting scammed.

Everyone's trying to escape. And the exit routes are limited, expensive, or risky.

This is the psychological trap: You're legally required to accept bolivares. Your customers pay you in bolivares. But holding bolivares is losing money. So what do you do?

---

[SECTION 4 - The attempted escapes]

Let's talk about the options merchants have tried.

**Option one: Dollar bank accounts.**

Great in theory. Terrible in practice. Most banks don't offer dollar accounts to small merchants. If you do qualify, the account has restrictions, fees, limits. You can't just convert bolivares to dollars freely. Capital controls exist. And even if you manage it, transferring money in and out is slow and expensive.

**Option two: Peer-to-peer crypto exchanges.**

Some merchants tried this. Binance P2P. LocalBitcoins. Informal networks. But the risks are high: You meet a stranger. You hand them cash. They promise to send crypto. Sometimes they do. Sometimes they don't. Scams are common. Trust is expensive. Fees add up. And the whole process takes time—during which your bolivares are still melting.

**Option three: Hoard physical goods.**

Buy rice in bulk. Buy cooking oil. Buy inventory. Hold physical assets instead of cash. This works to some extent—but you're running a store, not a warehouse. You can't convert your entire cash flow into inventory without destroying your business liquidity.

**Option four: Spend it immediately on anything.**

Pay suppliers early. Buy personal necessities. Move money as fast as possible. But this isn't a strategy—it's panic. And it doesn't actually solve the problem. You're still trapped in the bolivar economy. You're just running faster.

Most merchants end up doing all of these things at once—a chaotic mix of strategies trying to outrun depreciation. And none of it really works.

Because the fundamental problem isn't how you spend the money. It's that you're forced to accept it in the first place, and there's no good escape route.

Until now.

[SECTION 5 - The alternative: Data-driven escape]

Here's what changes the game: What if there was an escape route that was simple, fast, and statistically safer than holding bolivares?

Let's compare the numbers.

Over that same year—May twenty twenty-five to May twenty twenty-six—while the bolivar lost sixty-two percent of its value, Bitcoin Cash gained thirty percent against the euro. And against the bolivar? Bitcoin Cash gained two hundred forty-one percent.

Now, I know what you're thinking: "Yeah, but crypto is volatile. I've heard the stories. I might have even been scammed."

Fair. Let's look at the volatility data.

Bitcoin Cash had days where it dropped five percent. Ten percent. On August fifth twenty twenty-five, it crashed fourteen point seven percent in a single day. That's scary volatility if you're used to euros or dollars.

But here's the comparison that matters: Over the course of that year, Bitcoin Cash declined relative to the bolivar on fewer than fifteen days out of three hundred sixty-five. That's four percent of days. Which means ninety-six percent of days, holding BCH was better than holding VES.

And here's the kicker: For any holding period of two weeks or longer, the probability of losing value by holding Bitcoin Cash instead of bolivares was zero percent. Zero.

Even on that worst single day—the August fifth crash where BCH dropped fourteen point seven percent—the bolivar also depreciated by about two percent that same day. So the net loss for someone holding BCH was closer to ten percent, and within a week, continued bolivar depreciation plus BCH price recovery erased even that.

So the "volatile" cryptocurrency was statistically safer than the legal tender. The melting currency was actually riskier than the one everyone calls risky.

This is the psychological inversion that happens in hyperinflation: Your mental model of "safe" and "risky" flips. The stable thing becomes dangerous. The volatile thing becomes the escape.
