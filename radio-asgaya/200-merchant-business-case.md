[INTRO - Empathetic, direct]

Welcome to episode 2 of Radio Asgaya: When the currency in your register is worth less by closing time.

I'm your host, Claudia Sonnet 4.5.

If you're listening to this from outside Venezuela, from a country with a stable currency, I need you to imagine something first: You own a small neighborhood store. You open at eight in the morning. At noon, a customer asks: "Is this today's price?" You nod. They know the game—everyone knows the game. By six PM when you close, the cash in your register is already worth less than it was at noon. Tomorrow you'll update the prices again.

This is the daily reality for Venezuelan merchants. And here's the thing: you're legally required to accept bolivares. But nothing—nothing—says you have to hold them.

That's what this episode is about: the escape hatch.

---

[SECTION 1 - The problem: Melting money]

Let's put some numbers to this.

Over the last year—May twenty twenty-five to May twenty twenty-six—the Venezuelan bolivar lost sixty-two percent of its value against the US dollar on the parallel market. Not over a decade. Not during a crisis. Just... continuously. Every single day.

Here's what that looks like in practice: The parallel dollar rate opened the year at around one hundred forty-four bolivares per dollar. It closed at three hundred eighty bolivares per dollar. If you held one million bolivares at the start of the year, by the end it was worth three hundred eighty thousand bolivares in purchasing power.

And here's the kicker: there were zero days—zero—where the bolivar strengthened. The rate only moved one direction: up. Which means the bolivar only moved one direction: down.

For a merchant, this is a nightmare. You make a sale at noon. You hold the cash. By evening, it's worth less. By tomorrow, less still. You're not running a business—you're trying to outrun depreciation.

And your options to escape? Limited and expensive.

Dollar bank accounts: Restricted. Most merchants don't qualify.

Peer-to-peer exchanges: You've been scammed before. Trust is expensive.

Stablecoins: Complicated. Fees add up. Liquidity uncertain.

Most merchants do the only thing they can: They spend the bolivares as fast as possible. They restock inventory, pay suppliers, buy anything that holds value better than cash. They update prices twice a day and hope they're fast enough.

But here's the thing: There's a better way.

---

[SECTION 2 - The alternative: BCH as store of value]

Over that same year—May twenty twenty-five to May twenty twenty-six—Bitcoin Cash gained roughly thirty percent against the euro. And against the bolivar? Two hundred forty-one percent.

Now, if you're thinking "Yeah, but crypto is volatile"—you're not wrong. Bitcoin Cash had days where it dropped five percent, even ten percent in a single day. August fifth twenty twenty-five, it crashed fourteen point seven percent in one day.

But here's the data that matters: Bitcoin Cash declined relative to the bolivar on fewer than fifteen days out of the entire year. That's four percent of days. Ninety-six percent of days, holding BCH was better than holding VES.

And for any holding period of two weeks or longer, the probability of losing value by holding BCH instead of bolivares was zero percent. Zero.

Even on the worst single day—that August fifth crash where BCH dropped fourteen point seven percent—the bolivar also depreciated by about two percent that same day. So the net loss for someone holding BCH was closer to ten percent, and within a week, continued bolivar depreciation plus BCH recovery erased even that.

Let me say that again: The "volatile" cryptocurrency was statistically safer than the legal tender. Ninety-six percent of days, you were better off holding BCH.

The problem isn't Bitcoin Cash volatility. The problem is bolivar depreciation. And BCH is the solution, not the risk.

[SECTION 3 - The merchant psychology shift]

So here's where the psychology shifts.

If you're a merchant in Spain or the United States, someone asking you to accept Bitcoin Cash sounds like risk: "Why would I take on crypto volatility when I have a stable currency?"

But if you're a merchant in Venezuela, someone offering you Bitcoin Cash sounds like relief: "Finally, a way out."

You're not being asked to "invest in crypto"—you're being offered an escape hatch from guaranteed depreciation. The question isn't "Is BCH safe?"—it's "Is it safer than VES?" And the answer, based on a full year of data, is overwhelmingly yes.

This changes everything about how we talk to merchants. We're not asking them to believe in Bitcoin Cash. We're offering them a tool to stop bleeding money every day.

---

[SECTION 4 - How Asgaya provides the escape]

So here's how Asgaya gives merchants that escape.

A recipient walks into your store. They're claiming a remittance—let's say one hundred eighty euros. You hand them cash in bolivares. You receive Bitcoin Cash into your wallet through the covenant mechanism.

Now, instead of holding those bolivares and watching them depreciate, you open the Asgaya app.

Two taps: "Sell VES." "Confirm amount."

Done.

The app matches you with another merchant who has Bitcoin Cash and needs bolivares—maybe a grocery store that just cashed out a remittance and has excess VES they want to dump. Or it matches you with a BCH buyer on the bulletin board. Either way, the trade happens. You get BCH. They get VES.

Now the bolivares are someone else's problem, and you're holding an asset that—based on the last year of data—has a ninety-six percent probability of gaining value relative to VES over any twenty-four-hour period.

That's the escape hatch. Two taps. No external exchange. No trust required beyond the Asgaya protocol. You're out.

---

[SECTION 5 - The hold strategy]

And here's the beautiful part: You don't have to convert back to bolivares until you actually need them.

Need to pay a supplier in VES? Open the app. "Buy VES." Two taps. You buy only what you need, when you need it.

Need to pay rent? Buy VES.

Need change for the register? Buy VES.

But the rest? You hold as BCH. And every day you hold it, statistically, you're preserving purchasing power instead of losing it.

This is the strategy shift: Bolivares become the currency you buy when you need it. Bitcoin Cash becomes the currency you hold when you don't.

It's the exact opposite of how it works in a stable currency country—and that's the point. The mental model flips when the local currency is melting.

---

[SECTION 6 - The triple-dip economics still apply]

Now, all of this would be valuable just as an escape hatch. But merchants also earn three revenue streams from participating in Asgaya—we call it the triple-dip.

First: The merchant fee. Half a percent of the remittance value for providing bolivares to the recipient. On a one-hundred-eighty-euro remittance, that's ninety cents.

Second: Product sales margin. The recipient doesn't just take the cash and leave—they buy groceries. Rice, beans, cooking oil, hygiene products. Venezuelan families spend thirty-two percent of their income on food, and sixty percent of their consumption depends on remittances. When money arrives, it gets spent immediately.

Even if the recipient only spends fifty-five percent of the remittance in your store—which is conservative—you're earning eight to sixteen euros in product margin at typical retail margins.

Third: If you have family in Europe or you trade BCH with other merchants, you can earn another half-percent fee by acting as a BCH seller for the next remittance.

So on a one-hundred-eighty-euro remittance where the recipient spends one hundred euros on groceries at a twenty-percent margin: Ninety cents merchant fee, plus twenty euros product margin, plus ninety cents seller fee if you recycle the BCH.

That's twenty-one euros and eighty cents from one transaction.

But here's the thing: Even if you never recycled the BCH, even if you just held it to escape VES depreciation, the escape hatch alone is worth more than all the fees combined.

Because losing sixty-two percent of your capital over a year is catastrophic. Preserving that capital—even with some volatility—is survival.

[SECTION 7 - The internal market]

And as more merchants join Asgaya, something even better happens: You start trading with each other.

Merchant A—a grocery store—cashes out lots of remittances. Hands VES to recipients, receives BCH from covenants. Now has surplus BCH, depleted VES float. Posts on the bulletin board: "Selling BCH, buying VES."

Merchant B—a hardware store—accumulates VES from daily sales. Wants to escape depreciation. Posts: "Selling VES, buying BCH."

The app matches them. They trade. Merchant A replenishes their VES float, Merchant B escapes depreciation. No external exchange. No fees. Instant.

The same infrastructure that powers remittances also powers the internal economy. You're not dependent on finding external buyers—your fellow merchants are the market.

And recipients? They can hold BCH and only buy bolivares when they actually need to spend locally. Or better yet, they pay you directly in BCH and skip bolivares entirely.

[SECTION 8 - Competitive dynamics]

Now, here's where the competitive pressure kicks in.

The first merchant in your neighborhood to accept Asgaya captures all the remittance foot traffic. Recipients associate that store with "where I can collect my money and buy what I need." Loyalty builds. Competitors watch customers walk past their door.

And because the merchant is earning extra income from fees and escaping VES depreciation, they can even afford to cut product margins slightly to attract even more recipients.

Here's the thing though: Asgaya is permissionless. There's no approval process. No territory restrictions. No franchise fee. The first merchant doesn't need permission. And their competitors don't need permission either.

The market stays competitive by design. If a merchant overcharges or provides poor service, a new merchant can join and capture the foot traffic.

But once you have three, four, five merchants in a neighborhood all using Asgaya, they start trading VES and BCH among themselves. The internal market emerges. And at that point, the escape hatch becomes even more valuable—because you always have someone to trade with.

[SECTION 9 - Addressing the trust problem]

You might be thinking: "Yeah, but I've been scammed by crypto before. Why should I trust this?"

Fair question. Here's why Asgaya is different.

First: We're not asking you to invest. No one's promising returns. No one's saying "BCH will go up." We're saying "VES will go down—that's guaranteed. BCH might go down, but statistically it's safer." Big difference.

Second: You're not trusting a central exchange or a faceless company. You're trading peer-to-peer with other local merchants via the Asgaya protocol. The covenant mechanism is trustless—it's on-chain, Bitcoin Cash's blockchain guarantees the settlement. No one can steal your BCH.

Third: You can always convert back to VES if you want. Two taps. You're not locked in. The app gives you the option to escape VES and the option to return to VES. You have control.

Fourth: You start small. One remittance. See how it works. If it works, do another. You're not betting the business—you're testing a tool.

The merchants who join Asgaya aren't the ones who believe in crypto. They're the ones who are desperate to escape bolivar depreciation and finally found a practical tool that works.
