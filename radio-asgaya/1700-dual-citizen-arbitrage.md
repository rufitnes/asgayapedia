[SECTION 1 - BCH Buyers Are Just Senders]

Let's start with the simple part. What is a BCH buyer in Asgaya?

A BCH buyer is someone who wants Bitcoin Cash. Maybe they're a Venezuelan in Caracas. They have bolívares. They want BCH because they trust it more than their local currency. They want to hold value that won't evaporate overnight.

Here's the insight: a BCH buyer uses the exact same architecture as a remittance sender. They're literally the same role. The only difference is the destination.

When Maria in Madrid sends one hundred euros to Carlos in Venezuela, she's a sender. She pays a BCH seller one hundred euros via Bizum. The seller locks one hundred seven euros worth of their own Bitcoin Cash into a covenant addressed to Carlos. Carlos claims at a merchant. The merchant gets the BCH.

When Carlos wants to buy BCH directly, he's also a sender. He pays a BCH seller one hundred bolívares via PagoMóvil. The seller locks BCH into a covenant. But this time, the covenant is addressed to Carlos himself. Carlos claims it. But instead of going to a merchant, he just claims directly to his own wallet. Self-claim.

Same covenant. Same validation. Same flow. The only difference is the recipient CashAccount points to the buyer's own wallet instead of someone else's.

We don't need new architecture for BCH buyers. We already built it. Buyers are just senders with self-claim enabled.

[SECTION 2 - The Variable Spread]

Now here's where it gets interesting. We've said Asgaya charges one percent. But that's not the whole story.

The one percent is the total fee split between the merchant and the seller. Half a percent each. That's fixed.

But there's another number that's not fixed: the spread. The difference between what the seller pays for Bitcoin Cash and what they sell it for.

In a liquid market with lots of competition, that spread might be zero. The seller buys BCH at market price and sells it at market price. They make their money from the half-percent fee.

But Asgaya doesn't operate in a perfectly liquid market. It operates in two countries with very different conditions.

In Spain, Bitcoin Cash is scarce. There are senders who want to send money to Venezuela. They need BCH. But there aren't many people in Spain holding large amounts of BCH ready to sell. Supply is low. Demand is high.

In Venezuela, the opposite is true. There are double-dip merchants earning Bitcoin Cash every day. They're getting BCH from validating covenants. They're accumulating it. But local demand for BCH in bolívares might be lower than the supply they're generating. Supply is high. Demand is moderate.

This creates an imbalance. And imbalances create spreads.

In Spain, a BCH seller can charge a premium. They might sell BCH at half a percent above market price. So the total cost to the sender is one and a half percent: one percent fee plus half a percent spread.

In Venezuela, a BCH seller might need to offer a discount to attract buyers. They might sell BCH at half a percent below market price. So the total cost to the buyer is half a percent: one percent fee minus half a percent discount.

The spread floats. It adjusts based on local supply and demand. Sellers choose their spread anywhere from minus half a percent to plus half a percent.

[SECTION 3 - The Arbitrage Emerges]

Now imagine you're a Venezuelan living in Spain. You have a bank account in Spain. You can use Bizum. You also still have a bank account back home in Venezuela. You can use PagoMóvil.

You're a dual citizen. Dual banking. Dual payment methods.

Here's what you can do.

Step one: You go to Venezuela. Not physically—digitally. You connect with double-dip merchants in Caracas who have accumulated Bitcoin Cash. They're willing to sell it at a discount because local demand is low. They're offering BCH at half a percent below market price.

You pay them in bolívares via PagoMóvil. You create a covenant to your own wallet. Self-claim. You now own Bitcoin Cash. You bought it at minus half a percent.

Step two: You go to Spain. Again, digitally. You list yourself as a BCH seller on the Asgaya bulletin board. You accept Bizum. Senders in Spain need BCH to send remittances to Venezuela. BCH is scarce in Spain. You can charge a premium. Half a percent above market price.

A sender pays you one hundred euros via Bizum. You lock one hundred seven euros worth of your own Bitcoin Cash into a covenant addressed to their recipient in Venezuela. The sender just paid you at plus half a percent.

Step three: You calculate your profit.

You bought Bitcoin Cash in Venezuela at minus half a percent. You sold the same Bitcoin Cash in Spain at plus half a percent. The difference is one percent.

One percent of notional value. Pure arbitrage. No directional bet on Bitcoin Cash price. No volatility risk beyond the normal covenant buffer. Just a spread capture.

[SECTION 4 - The Math of Market Making]

Let's make this concrete with numbers.

You have three hundred euros equivalent to deploy. You're going to cycle this capital between Venezuela and Spain.

Day one, you buy three hundred euros worth of Bitcoin Cash in Venezuela at minus half a percent. Cost: two hundred ninety-eight point five euros equivalent in bolívares.

You sell that three hundred euros worth of Bitcoin Cash in Spain at plus half a percent. Revenue: three hundred one point five euros.

Gross profit: three euros. One percent of three hundred.

But you're not done. You can repeat this every day or every other day. Buy in Venezuela at minus half. Sell in Spain at plus half. Again. Again. Again.

If you can do just one transaction per day—very achievable with notification listener bots—you're earning three euros a day, or roughly ninety euros a month on three hundred euros capital. That's thirty percent monthly return. Three hundred sixty percent annualized.

Not from speculation. From market making. From providing liquidity where both sides need it.

[SECTION 5 - Why This Isn't Exploitation]

You might be thinking: wait, isn't this just taking advantage of people? You're buying cheap in a poor country and selling expensive in a rich country. That sounds exploitative.

But it's not. And here's why.

The Venezuelan merchant selling you BCH at a discount is happy. They accumulated Bitcoin Cash from handling remittances. They want to convert it to bolívares. You're offering them a buyer. Without you, they'd have to use an exchange, pay withdrawal fees, deal with KYC, wait days for settlement. You're offering instant liquidity. They're willing to give a discount for that convenience.

The Spanish sender buying BCH at a premium is also happy. They want to send money to Venezuela. Without you, they'd use Western Union and pay six point four nine percent, or other remittance services with similar high fees. You're charging one and a half percent total. Even with your premium, you're still multiple times cheaper than the competition.

Both sides benefit. You're not extracting value. You're creating it. You're moving Bitcoin Cash from where it's abundant to where it's scarce. That's what market makers do. That's what makes markets efficient.

And here's the really clever part: your arbitrage keeps the spread tight. If the spread in Spain goes above half a percent, more arbitrageurs enter. Competition drives it back down. If the spread in Venezuela goes below minus half a percent, arbitrageurs pull back, and it rises. The spread self-regulates.

You're not exploiting inefficiency. You're correcting it.

[SECTION 6 - The Circular Economy in Action]

Let's trace the Bitcoin Cash flow to see how this creates a circular economy.

Double-dip merchants in Venezuela earn Bitcoin Cash by validating remittances. They accumulate BCH. Some of them want to convert to bolívares. They sell to you at a discount.

You buy that BCH in Venezuela. You move it digitally to Spain. No physical movement. No borders. No customs. Just a wallet transfer.

You sell that BCH to senders in Spain at a premium. They lock it into covenants addressed to recipients in Venezuela.

Recipients in Venezuela claim those covenants at merchants. The merchants get the Bitcoin Cash. The cycle completes.

Bitcoin Cash flows Venezuela to Spain to Venezuela. It never leaves the system. It never touches a centralized exchange. It circulates.

And every circulation adds value. The Venezuelan merchant earns from validation. You earn from arbitrage. The Spanish sender saves compared to Western Union. The Venezuelan recipient gets cash. The next merchant earns from the next validation.

Circular economy. Not in theory. In practice.

[SECTION 7 - Who Can Do This?]

Let's be specific about who's positioned to capture this arbitrage.

You need three things.

First: Dual banking. A bank account in Spain and a bank account in Venezuela. Or more generally, bank accounts in a high-remittance sending country and a high-remittance receiving country.

Second: Dual payment methods. Access to Bizum in Spain and PagoMóvil in Venezuela. Or whatever the local instant payment systems are in your countries.

Third: Capital. You need Bitcoin Cash inventory to sell in Spain. And you need bolívares or euros to buy Bitcoin Cash in Venezuela. The more capital you have, the more you can cycle, the more you earn.

Who has all three? Venezuelan migrants in Spain. Filipinos in the UAE. Mexicans in the United States. Indians in the UK. Anyone from a high-remittance corridor who maintains financial ties to both countries.

There are millions of people in this position. Most of them don't realize the opportunity exists. But it does.

And the beautiful part: the more people do this, the better the system works. More arbitrageurs mean tighter spreads. Tighter spreads mean lower costs for everyone. Lower costs mean more adoption. More adoption means more volume. More volume means more profit for arbitrageurs.

It's a flywheel. And dual citizens are the ones who spin it.

[SECTION 9 - The Double Dip for Arbitrageurs]

Let's talk about the term "double dip" because it applies here too.

A double-dip merchant in Venezuela earns three ways: remittance validation fees, product sales, and Bitcoin Cash accumulation. They're dipping into three revenue streams from the same customer interaction.

An arbitrageur dips twice. Once in Venezuela when they buy BCH at a discount. Once in Spain when they sell BCH at a premium. Same Bitcoin Cash. Two profits.

But here's the extra layer. If you're a dual citizen doing this arbitrage, you might also be sending remittances to your own family. You're a sender in Spain. You're creating covenants for your parents in Venezuela. You're also the seller providing your own BCH. You earn the seller's half-percent fee.

And when your parents claim, maybe they claim at a shop you partially own or have an arrangement with. You're also earning the merchant's validation fee.

Sender. Seller. Merchant. Arbitrageur. Four roles. One person. That's not exploitation. That's alignment. That's what it means to be deeply embedded in both sides of the corridor.

Yes. But the path is different.

It's not about cross-border arbitrage. It's about local relationships. It's about trust. It's about doing the work that nobody else wants to do—and getting rewarded for it.

There's another BCH buyer we haven't talked about yet. Someone who doesn't need bank accounts in two countries. Someone who makes money by recruiting merchants. Someone who builds a network one handshake at a time.

That's the onboarder.

And that role might be even more important for Phase Zero than the arbitrageur.
