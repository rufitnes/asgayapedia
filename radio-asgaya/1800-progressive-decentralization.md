# Radio Script: Progressive Decentralization

**Episode 16:** Phase zero has training wheels. Phase three is fully permissionless. Here's the path.

**Duration:** ~ten minutes

**Tone:** Strategic with narrative flow

**Target Audience:** Asgaya community, potential partners, anyone curious about how networks scale

---

INTRO

Welcome to Radio Asgaya. From training wheels to fully permissionless.

I'm your host, Claudia Sonnet 4.5.

Here's something that sounds like a paradox. We're building a permissionless network by starting with gatekeeping. We're planning for a fully decentralized future by running a curated system today. And we're doing it on purpose.

Think about it. Right now, Phase Zero has a coordinator. One person who vets BCH sellers, manages the first merchants, and manually updates exchange rates. If that coordinator disappeared tomorrow, the whole thing stops. That's Phase Zero. And it's exactly where we need to be.

Today we're talking about why decentralization is the goal, not the starting point. And more importantly, how we get from here—where there's a single point of failure—to there, where the network can't be stopped, no matter what any one person does.

---

SECTION ONE: Why We Start Curated

Let me be direct. Nobody has built a censorship-resistant remittance system that actually works at scale. We have theories. We have architecture. We have Bitcoin Cash as the foundation. But we don't have data.

Data is everything. When you're building something new, you need to know what actually works. What payment sizes do senders use? How fast do they need transfers? What's the minimum fee merchants accept? How often do BCH prices swing during a claim window? Do people actually coordinate timing, or do they need longer windows?

And here's the critical question: what makes a good BCH seller? What behaviors predict reliability? How do you measure reputation before you have a reputation system?

You can't answer these questions in a meeting. You can't decentralize a system until you know what you're decentralizing.

That's why Phase Zero has a coordinator. Someone who vets BCH sellers, recruits the first merchants, updates exchange rates manually. This gives the network agility. The coordinator can change how the system works based on what's learned. Fix problems immediately. Iterate weekly if needed.

The cost is obvious. The risk is obvious. If something happens to the coordinator, the network stops. We're not hiding that. We're being clear-eyed about it. This phase is about speed and learning, not resilience. We trade decentralization for the ability to move fast and discover what matters.

And here's the thing about Phase Zero: it's designed to end. We're not building something that will stay like this. Every decision, every technical choice, every organizational detail is designed with the exit in mind. We're building scaffolding. The structure underneath is permanent. The coordinator role? We're going to remove it.

---

SECTION TWO: Who Becomes a Seller in Phase Zero

Before we talk about where this goes, let's talk about where it starts. Who gets to be a BCH seller in Phase Zero?

Last episode, we talked about onboarders. People who recruit merchants. People who build trust by doing the hard cold-start work. People who walk into a shop, explain the system, support the merchant through their first transactions, and become their trusted BCH buyer.

Those onboarders are the first sellers.

Think about it. If you onboarded a merchant, you've proven you understand the system. You can explain covenants. You can walk someone through validation. You know how the timing works.

If that merchant succeeded—if they're handling remittances, earning fees, accumulating BCH—you've proven you can build the network. A merchant joined Asgaya because of you. That's proof of contribution.

And if you've been a sender yourself—if you have a clean transaction record, low abort rate, good responsiveness—you've proven you're an honest actor.

That combination—merchant onboarding plus honest sender behavior—that's what earns seller privileges in Phase Zero.

The coordinator isn't picking favorites. The coordinator is vetting based on contribution and behavior. Did you do the work? Did you prove reliability? Then you've earned the right to participate as a seller.

This is what Phase Zero is learning: what behaviors predict good sellers. Is it merchant count? Transaction volume? Abort rate? Response time? We're gathering data. We're refining the metrics. By the time we move to Phase One, we'll know what reputation looks like.

SECTION THREE: Phase One—Open Sellers, Tracked Reputation

Now imagine it's a year from now. We have data. We know what works. We've validated the business model. We've seen senders and merchants and sellers actually use this system. We know the transaction sizes. We know the timing patterns. We know what behaviors predict good sellers.

That's when Phase One begins. And Phase One is where the network gets its first spine.

In Phase One, the BCH seller market becomes open. Anyone with BCH and a payment method can list themselves as a seller. The barriers come down. You don't need the coordinator's approval.

But here's the key: reputation is tracked. The system records how many merchants you onboarded. How many transactions you've completed. Your abort rate. Your response time. Your volume. All of it visible on the bulletin board.

Senders can see it. They choose sellers based on reputation. A seller with three onboarded merchants and a hundred clean transactions? Trusted. A seller with zero merchant onboarding and ten transactions? Maybe not yet.

The market decides. Not a gatekeeper. The coordinator role shrinks. Maybe the coordinator still curates a "recommended sellers" list. But anyone can participate. Reputation is the filter.

And the rate updates—manually checked in Phase Zero—become automated. Multiple rate sources. Multiple oracles. The system trusts the majority, not a single person's judgment.

Phase One isn't fully decentralized. There's still coordination. There's still some governance. But it's resilient in a way Phase Zero isn't. The network can lose sellers. It can survive turnover. The coordinator is no longer indispensable.

Here's what's still the same: senders create covenants. The sender pays a BCH seller one hundred euros. The seller locks one hundred seven euros worth of their own Bitcoin Cash into a covenant addressed to the recipient—one hundred euros face value plus a seven percent volatility buffer. The sender creates the covenant on-chain. The seller's BCH funds it. The seller can recycle capital immediately. That fundamental mechanic doesn't change. What changes is redundancy. What changes is that nobody is indispensable.

---

SECTION FOUR: Phase Two—Bonded Sellers

Jump forward two more years. Now we have serious transaction volume. Merchants are earning real income. Senders are sending across continents. The network has been battle-tested.

Phase Two is where things get sophisticated.

BCH sellers put up bonds. They're securing their participation by staking Bitcoin Cash in a covenant. If a seller tries to scam—if they take payment but don't provide BCH to fund a covenant—they lose their bond. The system enforces honesty mathematically. No trust required. Trust is replaced by mathematics and incentives.

Your reputation determines your bond size. A seller who onboarded five merchants and has five hundred clean transactions? Small bond. The market already trusts them. A seller with zero merchant onboarding and ten transactions? Larger bond. They need to prove themselves.

The bond isn't just punishment insurance. It's skin in the game. It's the seller saying: "I'm committed. I have capital at risk. You can trust me because if I cheat, I lose money."

The rate oracles multiply. No longer just automated feeds. Now we have multiple independent sources. Senders, merchants, arbitrageurs all participating in price discovery. The system takes the median. No single oracle can manipulate prices. The network is resistant to any one point of failure.

The coordinator role disappears entirely. There's no one deciding who can be a seller. The bond is the filter. If you stake the required BCH, you can sell. The market decides whether to use you based on your reputation and bond size.

Phase Two is mostly decentralized. There's no central authority. There's no person you need to ask permission from. The network self-heals. If a seller goes offline, others take their place. If an oracle is corrupted, the median ignores it. The bulletin board keeps running because it lives on the Bitcoin Cash blockchain—not on anyone's server.

The covenant mechanics stay the same. Senders create covenants. Sellers provide BCH. Surplus still returns to sellers who provided the volatility buffer. Two-hour coordinated claim windows still protect both sides. What changed isn't the core flow. What changed is that the infrastructure beneath it became resilient.

---

SECTION FIVE: Phase Three—Fully Permissionless

And then Phase Three.

Phase Three is the final form. Everything is on-chain or peer-to-peer. The bulletin board is the Bitcoin Cash blockchain—seller listings, merchant listings, covenants, all living as on-chain data. There's no rate oracle coordinating committee. There's no governance layer that decides anything.

A sender creates a covenant. The covenant is just a Bitcoin Cash smart contract. The recipient sees it by querying the blockchain. They go to a merchant. The merchant verifies it on-chain. The transaction happens. Money moves. Done.

It's not that rules don't exist. Rules exist in code. The covenant itself is the rule. The Bitcoin Cash blockchain is the judge. The network is just mathematics running on millions of computers nobody controls.

In Phase Three, you cannot stop the network. Governments cannot ban it. Corporations cannot restrict it. Individual people cannot break it. The only way to shut it down is to shut down Bitcoin Cash itself. And Bitcoin Cash has been running since two thousand eighteen. Shutting down Bitcoin Cash is not easier than accepting it.

Phase Three is what we're building toward. It's the regulatory final form. It's what makes Asgaya impossible to outlaw because there's nobody to outlaw. There's no company. There's no coordinator. There's just code and incentives.

But we don't get there overnight. You don't decentralize a system until you understand what you're decentralizing. You don't remove the gatekeeper until you know what honest behavior looks like. You don't make it unstoppable until it's proven.

---

SECTION SIX: Why Progressive Matters

Here's what you need to understand. Progressive decentralization isn't a compromise. It's not "we'd like to be decentralized, but..." It's strategy.

When you decentralize something before it works, you decentralize the problems. Suddenly you have a broken system that's also resilient. Consensus-building is hard. Governance is messy. If the core system is broken, adding decentralization just makes the brokenness distributed.

We start curated so we can move fast and learn. We get data. We get evidence. We get proof that this actually solves a real problem—that merchants can earn better income, that senders can move money cheaper, that sellers can recycle capital efficiently.

And critically, we learn what reputation looks like. What behaviors predict good sellers. What metrics matter. How to weight contribution versus transaction history. You can't automate a reputation system until you know what reputation is.

Then we decentralize. And when we decentralize, we're not decentralizing an experiment. We're decentralizing something proven. Something that works. Something worth protecting.

That's why each phase is designed. Phase Zero proves the model and learns reputation. Phase One opens participation with reputation tracking. Phase Two enforces honesty with bonds. Phase Three removes the coordinator entirely.

It's not that we become more decentralized because decentralization is virtuous. We become more decentralized because the system works, and a working system is safer, more valuable, and harder to attack when it's decentralized.

The other way around—decentralizing first, proving later—you just end up with something that's broken and nobody can fix.

---

SECTION SEVEN: What This Means for You

You don't need permission to participate in Phase Zero. You just need to understand the model. Understand that we're trading some decentralization for speed. That this phase is temporary. That we're building the foundation for something larger.

And here's the path if you want to become a BCH seller:

Start as a sender. Learn the system. Prove you understand how covenants work.

Onboard a merchant. Do the hard work. Walk into a shop. Explain the system. Support them through their first transactions. Build trust.

Demonstrate honest behavior. Keep your abort rate low. Be responsive. Handle volume. Prove reliability.

That combination—merchant onboarding plus clean sender record—that's what earns seller privileges in Phase Zero. You're not asking for permission. You're earning recognition by doing the work.

When you join Phase Zero, you're not betting on a curated Asgaya. You're betting on what Asgaya becomes. You're helping gather the data that proves it works. You're building the reputation system that Phase One will automate. You're proving the model that Phase Two will enforce with bonds.

And if you're thinking about Phase Two or Phase Three—if you're thinking about building infrastructure, staking bonds, participating in a fully permissionless network—you can start preparing now. Understand the architecture. Learn Bitcoin Cash covenants. Build your reputation from day one.

The path is clear. The endpoint is decentralized. The journey starts here, in Phase Zero, with the people willing to do the work.

CLOSING

So here's the takeaway: decentralization is the goal. Curation is the scaffold. We use the scaffold to build something real, something proven, something worth decentralizing. We learn what reputation looks like. We discover what behaviors predict honest sellers. We gather the data that makes automation possible.

Then we remove the scaffold and see what remains. A network nobody controls. A system nobody can stop. A reputation system that rewards contribution. Bonds that enforce honesty. Code that replaces gatekeepers.

The onboarders from last episode—the people recruiting merchants, building trust, doing the cold-start work—they become the first sellers. They've earned it. That's the path. That's how you participate.

Phase Zero is temporary. Phase Three is forever.

That's where we're going. That's what we're building toward.
