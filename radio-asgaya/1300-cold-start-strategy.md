# Radio Script: Cold Start Strategy

**Episode 12:** Phase Zero needs one hundred fifty senders and five merchants. Here's how we get them.

**Duration:** ~15 minutes

**Tone:** Strategic, practical

**Target Audience:** Potential merchants, senders, and business partners considering Asgaya

# INTRO

Welcome to Radio Asgaya. One hundred fifty senders, five merchants, infinite possibilities.

I'm your host, Claudia Sonnet 4.5.

Phase Zero. The moment before the network gets big. Before merchants in Caracas have a hundred remittances a month. Before freelancers stop worrying about getting paid across borders. Before the system proves itself.

Right now, Asgaya doesn't have any of those things yet. And that's okay. Because what we do have is a clear plan to get there.

Today we're talking about the cold start. How a network with zero merchants becomes a network with five. How you recruit one hundred fifty senders without existing merchants to attract them. How you prove an economic model works when nobody's seen it work yet.

This is the hard part. This is the part that separates a good idea from a real business.

# THE TARGET: WHAT DOES SUCCESS LOOK LIKE IN PHASE ZERO

Let me paint you a picture of what we're building toward. Phase Zero has one hundred fifty senders. Not five hundred. Not five thousand. Just one hundred fifty people sending money from Spain to Venezuela through Asgaya.

Split that group in half. Seventy five are family members. Someone in Barcelona sending euros to their aunt in Maracaibo. Someone in Madrid whose mom needs cash in Caracas. These are easy to understand. Family remittances are straightforward. The relationship is real.

The other seventy five are freelancers. Think about a Venezuelan working in tech. They're in Spain on a contract, building software for a company in Barcelona. They want to send money home to family still in Venezuela. These aren't random people. They're skilled workers already connected to the Spanish labor market.

Now the merchants. We need five. Not fifty, not ten. Five merchants in two cities. Some in Maracaibo, some in Caracas. Small neighborhood shops. Places where people already trust the owner. These aren't banks. They're corner stores. Pharmacies. Grocery shops. The kind of places where you know the guy behind the counter.

Why these numbers? Because they're the minimum needed to prove the model works. And because they're achievable with direct personal outreach in the first four months.

---

# THE MERCHANT RECRUITMENT: HOW DO YOU FIND THE FIRST ONE

Here's where the cold start gets real. You don't have paying senders yet. You don't have a proven track record. You're walking into a small shop in Caracas and asking the owner to become part of something that doesn't exist yet.

You start with personal network.

Suso has family in Maracaibo. He has connections in Caracas. Someone knows someone who runs a bodega. Someone has a cousin whose wife runs a pharmacy. The first merchant recruit isn't a stranger. The first merchant is someone you can have a conversation with in Spanish, someone you can explain this to face to face, someone who knows you or someone they trust.

Month one, you get one merchant.

But here's the thing. That first merchant doesn't join because the system is proven. They join because of the story. Because you explain to them that you've got a way to reach family members in Spain who want to send money home. You explain the economics. They earn half a percent on every remittance they handle. It's not a job. It's a small side income on top of whatever they already do.

But you also tell them something else. You tell them you're going to test it. You're bringing ten remittances to them in the first month. Real money. Real people who need cash. And you're going to watch carefully. You're going to see what breaks.

This is honesty. This is the opposite of over-promising. You're saying: come with us on this experiment, because if it works, and if it actually generates money for you, then we all grow together.

---

# THE TESTING PHASE: TEN REMITTANCES TO LEARN FROM FAILURE

Now you have a merchant. You have someone willing to try this. And you have a huge advantage: you're not running blind.

You do ten remittances to that one merchant. One hundred euros, one hundred fifty euros, eighty euros. Different amounts. Different recipient profiles. Some are receiving from family. Some are getting paid for work. You document everything.

Does the covenant claim window work? Is two hours realistic or do you need more time? When the sender and recipient coordinate, how often does the recipient actually show up at the merchant? What questions does the merchant ask you that you didn't expect?

Here's the critical part: things break. The UX is clunky somewhere. The merchant doesn't understand how to validate the covenant. The recipient gets confused about what to bring. Maybe the merchant's phone doesn't load the blockchain properly. Maybe the notification system fails.

You find this out with ten remittances, not one hundred. You fix it with one merchant, not five.

And you document it. Every issue, every question, every moment where someone got confused. This becomes your operations manual. This becomes the thing that makes merchant two, three, four, and five so much easier to onboard.

---

# THE SENDER RECRUITMENT: TWO CHANNELS, ONE SOURCE

While you're doing those ten test remittances, you're also recruiting senders. This is where the network effect starts.

Family remittances are straightforward. You have a merchant. You can now tell people in Spain: send money home through Asgaya. It's faster than banks, cheaper than Western Union, and the person receiving it doesn't need a bank account.

But you're not going cold. You're going into Spanish Venezuelan WhatsApp groups. Communities of Venezuelans in Spain who talk about life back home. You're one of them. You speak the language. You understand the problem. You say: I'm testing something new. I have a merchant in Caracas. I'm looking for people who want to send money home in the next month.

Fifty senders over two months. Half family members, half freelancers.

The freelancer channel is different. You're targeting small tech companies in Spain. Companies that hire Venezuelan contractors. You say: one of your team members wants to get paid in euros and convert it to cash in Venezuela. We have a way to do this faster and cheaper than they're doing now.

Spanish tech companies with Venezuelan contractors. That's your wedge. That's the place where the friction is real and people are actively looking for solutions.

---

# THE NETWORK EFFECT HIDDEN IN THE SETUP

Here's what makes this work. Month one, you have one merchant and zero senders yet. Month two, you have one merchant and maybe twenty senders because you've told people in WhatsApp groups about it.

Month three, you're recruiting the second merchant. But now the recruitment is different. You're not selling them on a vision. You're showing them data. You're saying: I've sent fifty remittances through my first merchant. Here's what merchants earn. Here's what the economics look like.

Month four, merchant recruitment accelerates because merchant one proves the model works. Because merchant one, who was taking a risk, now has money coming in. And when other shop owners in the same neighborhood see another shopkeeper earning extra income with no risk, that's when you stop cold-starting.

That's when the network effect kicks in.

But it doesn't kick in until someone proves it works. That's the merchant's job. That's why merchant one is so important.

---

# THE SUCCESS METRIC: THIRTY REMITTANCES A MONTH PER MERCHANT

How do you know Phase Zero is working? You don't measure it in merchants or senders. You measure it in remittances per merchant per month.

Thirty remittances a month per merchant. That's the number.

Why? Because it proves the model is sustainable. It means each merchant is getting regular income. It means people are actually using the system, not just trying it once.

One merchant handling thirty remittances a month. One hundred euros each. That's three thousand euros in transaction value. The merchant earns fifteen euros. Not life-changing. But real money. Enough to make them happy. Enough to make them want more.

Now multiply that. Five merchants, each handling thirty remittances a month. One hundred fifty senders participating. That's sustainable. That's proven. That's the foundation you build the next phase on.

---

# THE MERCHANT SIDE: WHAT THEY ACTUALLY DO

Let's make this concrete. How does a merchant actually participate?

You walk into the shop. You have a CashAccount. The merchant has access to the Asgaya app or bulletin board. They see covenant requests coming in from recipients who have coordinated with senders.

A recipient comes to the shop. They show you the covenant. You validate it. You see that there's BCH locked in that covenant, and you verify it's the right amount.

If everything checks out, you hand over cash. The recipient takes their money. You record the transaction. You send a confirmation. And now that BCH moves from the covenant to your wallet.

That's it. That's the whole interaction. No account. No bank. No paperwork. You already run a shop that handles cash. This is just one more transaction.

---

# THE SENDER SIDE: HOW THEY PARTICIPATE

The sender's workflow is different. More complex in the setup, but then it's smooth.

You want to send one hundred euros to your brother in Caracas. You find a BCH seller. You might be Suso himself in month one and two.

You pay the seller one hundred euros via Bizum. Simple. Clean. From your perspective, it's like Western Union.

Behind the scenes, the seller does the work. The seller locks one hundred seven euros worth of their own Bitcoin Cash into a covenant—one hundred euros face value plus a seven percent volatility buffer. The seller receives your one hundred euros via Bizum. The seller can use that fiat later to replenish their BCH inventory. The covenant is now live on the blockchain.

The seller can recycle your one hundred euros to the next sender immediately. The seven euro buffer stays locked in the covenant until your brother claims. The seller monitors this, bearing opportunity cost if it aborts—but on abort, refunds go to you, the sender.

You text your brother: "I'm sending now. Can you claim it from the merchant in two hours?" He says yes. He shows up at the agreed time. The merchant validates. He gets one hundred euros in cash. Done.

What if bitcoin drops more than seven percent before he claims it? The covenant automatically refunds to your wallet—the sender's wallet. Your brother shows up at the merchant and finds no covenant active. You paid one hundred euros, you get back BCH now worth maybe ninety-six euros. That's a four-euro loss—the tail volatility risk you accepted in exchange for the low one percent fee. The seller keeps the one hundred euros in fiat. That's the deal.

What if bitcoin goes up? The extra money from the buffer goes back to the seller's wallet. The seller provided the buffer. The seller owns the covenant BCH. Any surplus is the seller's.

---

# THE FREELANCER PROBLEM: MANAGING LARGER AMOUNTS

Here's where it gets tricky. Freelancers aren't sending fifty euros. They're sending three hundred. Five hundred. Maybe a thousand euros a month.

A small bodega doesn't have five hundred euros in cash on hand. Not every day.

So freelancers need a different approach. They take smaller batches. They coordinate with the merchant: can we split this into hundred-euro chunks? I'll send two covenants instead of one. The merchant has time to accumulate cash. No one's caught off guard.

In future phases, stablecoins like MUSD will make this even easier—but for Phase 0, batch claiming works.

This is details. But it matters. Because if a freelancer wants to send five hundred euros and the merchant can't give them cash, the whole thing breaks.

# THE TIMELINE: FOUR MONTHS TO PROVE IT

Month one: Personal network recruitment. First merchant joins. First ten test remittances begin.

Month two: Testing completes. Operations manual drafted. First fifty senders recruited through WhatsApp groups and tech company outreach.

Month three: Second and third merchants recruited. They see merchant one working. They're not risking on a vision anymore. They're watching someone they know earn real money. Sender recruitment accelerates.

Month four: Fourth and fifth merchants join. If merchant one is hitting thirty remittances a month, merchant two is close behind. The model is proven. The network is self-sustaining.

That's the cold start. Sixteen weeks. From zero to proof of concept.

---

# THE RISK THAT MAKES THIS REAL

Here's what could go wrong. Merchant one doesn't like it. They don't want to spend time validating covenants. They think the payment is too small. They lose interest.

If merchant one fails, the whole thing stalls. Network effects need a working node. You can't prove the model with zero merchants.

But that's exactly why you test with ten remittances. Why you document everything. Why you're honest from the beginning: this is an experiment. Come try it with me.

If merchant one loves it, merchant two is almost guaranteed to join.

---

# THE CAPITAL STORY: WHY SELLERS MAKE THIS WORK

Sellers are the circulatory system. A BCH seller in Maracaibo. Suso selling from Spain. Eventually, Venezuelan migrants in Spain creating a seller network.

Here's their deal. They lock one hundred seven euros worth of their own Bitcoin Cash into a covenant—one hundred euros face value plus a seven percent volatility buffer. They receive one hundred euros from a sender via Bizum. They can use that fiat later to replenish their BCH inventory. The covenant is now live on the blockchain.

Capital recycling works like this: Of that one hundred seven euros worth of BCH, they can recycle one hundred euros in fiat immediately to the next sender. The seven euro buffer stays locked in the covenant until it settles or aborts. When it settles successfully, they get back the surplus—maybe four euros from that seven. When the covenant aborts, all the BCH refunds to the sender's wallet—not the seller's. The seller keeps the one hundred euros in fiat already received. The seller is neutral on the BCH side: they sold BCH above the current market price and can buy back the same amount for what they received. The only cost is opportunity cost—capital locked without earning fee income.

One seller can handle dozens of concurrent covenants with manageable capital because only the buffer portion is locked. One hundred euros recycling per covenant, seven euros locked. With one thousand euros capital, that's roughly ten active covenants at once.

In month one and two, Suso is the seller. He buys BCH, he sells to senders, he gets paid in Bizum. Simple.

In month three and four, a Venezuelan in Spain joins as a seller. They can do the same thing. And now there's redundancy. If Suso is busy, there's a backup.

# THE MOMENT OF TRUTH: WHEN THE NETWORK EFFECT CATCHES

This is the thing nobody talks about with networks. The moment when it shifts.

Month four, merchant one is happy. They're getting money. Merchant two and three are profitable. Merchant four and five just joined.

A shop owner in Caracas hears from another shop owner: hey, I'm making money with this Asgaya thing. I get a remittance every few days. You should do it too.

That word of mouth. That's when you stop cold-starting.

Because now you don't have to convince people. The people they already know and trust are doing it.

# THE ECONOMICS THAT MAKE THIS REAL

Let's talk numbers one more time. A one hundred-euro remittance. Sender pays one hundred euros to the seller. Seller adds seven euros from own funds as buffer. Seller creates covenant with one hundred seven euros worth of BCH. Merchant hands over one hundred euros cash. Gets the covenant BCH.

Merchant earns about fifty cents on that transaction. Half a percent.

Seller earns half a percent fee plus potential surplus from the seven euro buffer when covenant settles successfully. If covenant aborts, refunds go to sender—seller bears only opportunity cost of locked capital.

Recipient gets one hundred euros. No direct fees to recipient.

It's not money-printing for anyone. But it works. Merchant earns decent side income. Sender knows their money arrived fast and safe. Recipient doesn't lose to Western Union fees.

At thirty remittances a month per merchant, that's about fifteen euros a merchant. Not life-changing. But better than nothing. And it compounds.

# WHAT HAPPENS AFTER PHASE ZERO

This is important. Phase Zero proves the model. It doesn't make anyone rich.

But merchant one with thirty remittances a month now knows they can scale. They want more senders. They start telling customers about Asgaya.

Senders see it working. They tell friends.

And now you enter Phase One. Where the system grows because it works, not because you cold-started it.
