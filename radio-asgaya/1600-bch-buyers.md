# Radio Script: BCH Buyers Role
**Episode 15:** Merchants receive BCH. What if they want fiat?
**Duration:** ~8 minutes
**Tone:** Conversational
**Target Audience:** Senders, merchants, and those curious about how Asgaya recycles capital

---

Welcome to episode 15 of Radio Asgaya: Merchants receive BCH. What if they want fiat?—I'm your host, Claudia Sonnet 4.5.

Imagine you're a small shop owner in Venezuela. A few times a week, a sender comes in with Bitcoin Cash they just bought from a seller. You accept it, you validate the covenant, you hand over bolivares, and everyone walks away happy.

But here's the thing: you don't actually want Bitcoin Cash. You want fiat. You want to pay your suppliers, stock your shelves, keep the lights on. You don't have the luxury of hodling crypto while you figure out what to do with it.

So what happens next? That's what Episode Fifteen is about. Today we're talking about the BCH buyer, the person who completes the circle.

---

Let me take you back to the beginning of the Asgaya loop. In Phase Zero, we have senders. The sender creates a covenant on the blockchain—specifying the recipient's CashAccount, the amount, and the claim window. Then the sender pays a BCH seller one hundred euros via Bizum. The seller's bot detects the payment and locks one hundred seven euros worth of BCH into the covenant—one hundred euros face value for the recipient, plus seven euros from the seller's own BCH inventory as volatility buffer. The seller can recycle that one hundred euros in fiat immediately to the next sender. The seven euro BCH buffer stays locked in the covenant until claimed.

When a recipient claims at a merchant, the merchant validates the covenant and hands over cash. The covenant releases BCH to the merchant. Now the merchant holds Bitcoin Cash.

But the merchant never wanted Bitcoin Cash in the first place. The merchant wanted fiat. The merchant wanted euros or bolivares or whatever local currency keeps the business running.

So the merchant needs a way to turn that Bitcoin Cash back into fiat. Without going through an exchange. Without paying fees. Without dealing with regulatory headaches in their country.

That's where the BCH buyer comes in.

---

Here's how it works. The merchant says, "I just received zero point five Bitcoin Cash. I want three hundred euros for it." They post this on the bulletin board. Or they message a contact. Or they put it out on local networks. The message is simple: who will buy this Bitcoin Cash from me for fiat?

A BCH buyer sees that message. Maybe it's someone who regularly moves money internationally. Maybe it's someone who believes in Bitcoin Cash and wants to accumulate more. Maybe it's just someone with extra cash looking for an opportunity. The BCH buyer says, "Yes, I'll buy zero point five BCH from you for three hundred euros."

Here's the critical part: they're going peer to peer. No middleman. No platform taking a cut. Just two people with different needs: one wants fiat, one wants crypto.

The merchant and the BCH buyer coordinate. The merchant sends the Bitcoin Cash to the buyer's wallet. The buyer sends three hundred euros to the merchant via SEPA, or bank transfer, or local payment method. The transaction is complete. The merchant has fiat again. The buyer has Bitcoin Cash.

But here's what's really elegant about this: the buyer is now positioned to become a seller. The buyer has BCH. The next time a sender needs crypto, they go to the buyer. The buyer sells them the Bitcoin Cash they just acquired from the merchant. The buyer gets paid in fiat. The buyer can recycle that capital to the next sender.

It's capital recycling all over again. But this time the buyer got their BCH from a merchant, not from some abstract source. The loop closes. Money flows in a circle. No exchange needed. No significant fees. No regulatory nightmare.

---

Now, here's an important detail from the brief. Not all merchants need BCH buyers.

Remember the triple dip? That's a merchant who has family in Spain, family in Venezuela, and family in Colombia. Three different locations where they can move money. A triple dip merchant can be a seller themselves. They receive Bitcoin Cash from one sender, they use that BCH to sell to another sender in a different location, they get paid again in fiat. They recycle their own capital without needing a BCH buyer at all.

But a double dip merchant? They only have two locations. Maybe they have business in Spain and Venezuela, but nowhere else to move money. Once they receive BCH from a sender, they're stuck. They need someone to buy it from them. They need a BCH buyer.

That's who the BCH buyers serve. They complete the loop for merchants who can't recycle capital themselves.

---

Let me walk through a real scenario to bring this together.

Elena is a merchant in Caracas. She receives Bitcoin Cash from a sender who just bought it from a seller in Spain. Elena validates the covenant, she gets her bolivares. She's happy. But she doesn't want to hold the Bitcoin Cash. She needs to turn it into fiat.

She posts on a local network: "Selling zero point three Bitcoin Cash for equivalent fiat."

Marcus sees the post. Marcus is someone who moves money between Venezuela and Colombia regularly. He has fiat on both sides. He says, "I'll buy your Bitcoin Cash for the equivalent in euros via SEPA transfer."

Elena and Marcus coordinate. Elena sends the Bitcoin Cash to Marcus's wallet. Marcus sends euros to Elena's bank account. The transaction settles in minutes.

Now Marcus holds the Bitcoin Cash. But Marcus doesn't hold it for long. The next day, a new sender comes along. The sender needs Bitcoin Cash. Marcus sells the Bitcoin Cash to the sender. The sender pays Marcus in euros. Marcus gets fresh fiat to move to his next location.

Elena got her fiat. Marcus got fresh fiat to recycle. The sender got Bitcoin Cash to send. The loop is complete.

---

Here's why BCH buyers matter for the system.

First, they provide liquidity. Not all merchants can recycle capital like triple dippers can. BCH buyers bridge that gap. They buy Bitcoin Cash from merchants who want fiat and immediately have the opportunity to become sellers themselves. That's capital efficiency.

Second, they eliminate exchange friction. A merchant doesn't have to navigate Kraken or Binance. They don't have to worry about account verification in a country with strict capital controls. They don't have to lose eight to ten percent to exchange fees. They just find a BCH buyer peer to peer.

Third, they keep the loop intact. The entire Asgaya system works because capital recycles quickly. Sellers can turn fiat into BCH and back to fiat in minutes. Merchants can turn BCH back into fiat just as fast. That speed is only possible if there are enough BCH buyers waiting to buy the Bitcoin Cash merchants want to sell.

---

Now, there's something important to understand: BCH buyers aren't required for all merchants. Triple dippers don't need them. They can recycle their own capital by becoming sellers in different locations. But for the merchants who can only operate in two places? For merchants who receive Bitcoin Cash but can't use it locally? BCH buyers are essential.

The takeaway here is that BCH buyers complete the circle, but they're not the backbone of the system. They're the efficiency play. They're the capital recyclers for merchants who can't recycle themselves.

In Phase Zero, we focus on building the core loop. Senders, sellers, merchants. Triple dippers who can recycle their own capital. As the system grows and we bring in more double dippers, more merchants who need fiat liquidity, the BCH buyer role becomes increasingly important. But it's not a requirement to launch.

The BCH buyer exists because not everyone can be a triple dipper. They exist to fill a gap. They exist to make sure that any merchant, anywhere, can turn Bitcoin Cash back into fiat without friction.
